#+TITLE: Autoresearch took our Pulumi preview from 300 seconds to 30
#+DATE: 2026-11-06
#+DRAFT: true
#+TAGS[]: ai pulumi
#+CATEGORIES[]: tech

At Oscilar, infrastructure changes go through a deployment service I wrote on top of Pulumi. Before a change ships, we preview it: Pulumi works out what would change in every stack and shows the diff. One environment is 26 stacks. The CI job that previews a change took about 5 minutes.

5 minutes is long enough to switch to something else and forget to come back. It's long enough that people skip the preview. A preview nobody runs catches nothing.

** The announcement

On March 12, Tobi Lütke [[https://x.com/tobi/status/2032212536716578932][posted]] that Shopify had open-sourced an autoresearch plugin for [[https://pi.dev][pi]], the coding agent: [[https://github.com/davebcn87/pi-autoresearch][pi-autoresearch]], written by David Cortés and inspired by Karpathy's autoresearch. "Just tell it what you want, it will do the rest."

The idea is simple. You give the agent a command and a number. It changes the code, runs the command, and reads the number. If the number got better, it commits. If not, it reverts. Then it tries something else, and it doesn't stop.

I had a slow command and a number. I installed it that night.

** Setting it up

The plugin's setup skill asks four questions. My answers:

1. *Goal:* get the preview as close to 15 seconds as possible.
2. *Command:* ~go run ./cmd/preview~, which previews every stack.
3. *Metric:* wall time in seconds, lower is better.
4. *Constraints:* don't change the stacks, the resources, or the state. Keep the preview's output the same.

From that it wrote an ~autoresearch.md~ with the objective, the files in scope and what was off limits, and an ~autoresearch.sh~ that ran the preview and printed one line:

#+begin_example
METRIC preview_run_seconds=330.439741
#+end_example

Every experiment was a commit or a revert, and every result went in a log. Over four days it logged 181 experiments: it kept 28, discarded 95, and 58 crashed.

** What the loop found

*** First it had to finish

The baseline didn't produce a number. Pulumi Cloud returned 401s, DNS lookups to Pulumi, AWS and the Kubernetes APIs failed, and stacks fell over halfway through.

So the first eleven experiments were all crashes. The loop tried fewer concurrent previews, fewer chart pulls, a different DNS resolver, and skipping Pulumi's update checks. Each crash got a little further. The cause was the preview tool opening 50 stacks at once, each with its own fresh Pulumi workspace, all hammering the same APIs.

Experiment 12 finished: one stack at a time, a pool of reused workspaces instead of a new one per stack, config written in one call instead of one call per key, and Helm charts pulled only for the stacks that use them. *330 seconds*, about five and a half minutes. That was the number to beat.

*** Then it got faster in steps

The stacks build on each other in waves: global, then regions, then clusters, then the services on each cluster. Earlier waves were flaky under load, and later waves weren't. So the loop ran the early waves one at a time and the service waves three at a time. *223 seconds.*

A trace showed about 34 seconds going to reading Pulumi config. Each stack read its whole config, wrote one key, and read it all back, just to merge one setting. Reading only that key, once per stack, up front, got it to *193 seconds.*

That was as far as it got against Pulumi Cloud. The runs were too noisy to trust a few seconds either way. Some runs died before starting because my laptop couldn't reach the cluster that holds the Pulumi credentials. At one point the agent changed my DNS to 8.8.8.8 to see if that helped. It didn't.

*** Changing the benchmark

I asked whether a Postgres backend would mean less remote work, and whether it could test that without touching the real stacks. It exported the 26 stacks, re-encrypted their secrets with a passphrase, loaded them into a local Postgres container, and pointed the preview at the copy. *137 seconds*, and much less noise.

To be clear about this one: that was a change to the benchmark, not to the code. Production still uses Pulumi Cloud. But a quiet benchmark is what made the next round of experiments possible, because a two-second win no longer disappeared in the noise.

*** Wave tuning

With a steady benchmark, the loop swept each wave's concurrency, one knob at a time, keeping what helped:

| Change                              | Seconds |
|-------------------------------------+---------|
| Clusters 2 at a time                |     126 |
| Clusters 3 at a time                |      98 |
| Regions 4 and global 2 at a time    |      83 |
| Service waves 4 to 5 at a time      |      78 |

Going higher on any one of them made it slower. Clusters at 4 lost. Services at 6 lost.

*** Turning off work nobody asked for

The two biggest single wins came from asking what work we could skip.

Every preview refreshed every stack first: it read every resource's live state from AWS and Kubernetes before diffing. The preview was there to show what a code change would do, so I told it to stop refreshing. *50 seconds.*

Then I asked the agent to dig through the Pulumi Go SDK for knobs. It found ~PULUMI_DISABLE_RESOURCE_REFERENCES~, which sends plain values instead of typed resource references between the program and the engine. *47 seconds*, and with less going on, more of each wave could run at once. Retuned, *38 seconds.*

*** What a trace said was left

I ran Pulumi's [[https://www.pulumi.com/docs/support/debugging/performance-tracing/][performance tracing]] across a full run. The time wasn't in diffing, which was under half a second in the traced stack. It was in reads: services rereading Kubernetes secrets they already had the values for, and stack references fetched again and again. The loop removed the safe rereads and resolved stack references once. That stayed around *38 seconds*, but with less load on the clusters.

The last run of the session: every wave at 50 at a time. *31.4 seconds.* At 100 it got slower again, 52.

** Results

| Stage                                        | Seconds |
|----------------------------------------------+---------|
| Baseline                                     | crashed |
| First run to finish                          |     330 |
| Wave-aware scheduling                        |     223 |
| Targeted config reads (best on Pulumi Cloud) |     193 |
| Local Postgres copy of the stacks            |     137 |
| Wave concurrency tuning                      |      78 |
| No refresh                                   |      50 |
| No resource references, retuned              |      38 |
| Every wave at 50                             |      31 |

From 330 seconds to 31: about five minutes to about thirty seconds. I didn't get to 15. Everything from 137 down was measured against the local Postgres copy; against Pulumi Cloud the best I measured was 193.

The changes went out in one PR: the workspace pool, the batched config, every wave at 50 by default, no refresh by default, and the trimmed secret and stack reference reads. Disabling resource references didn't ship. It's the riskiest of the changes, and it isn't set anywhere in our code or CI.

The CI job didn't drop the same way. It still takes a few minutes, but most of that is compiling the deployer and saving the Go module cache, not Pulumi.

** Lessons

*The loop is only as good as the number.* For the first day the number was mostly noise: DNS failures, expired tokens, a VPN that dropped. The loop discarded good ideas because a run landed on a bad minute. The targeted config read was tried and thrown out twice before it was kept on the third try. The local Postgres copy did more for the search than any single idea did. Next time I'd make the benchmark steady before starting the loop.

*Sweeping knobs is what it does best.* The wave concurrency sweep was dozens of runs I'd never have had the patience for. Try 3, try 4, keep 3, try the next wave. It's dull, it's easy to measure, and that's exactly what the loop wants.

*The big wins came from questions, not from the loop.* Stop refreshing. Test against a copy. Raise every wave to 50. Each came from me asking "why are we doing this at all?" or "what if?" The loop is good at climbing a hill. It doesn't ask whether it's on the right one.

*Read the profile early.* For the first day the agent was profiling by trial and error. When I asked if it had read the profiles, it said not really. When it did, they said the Go program was barely using the CPU. Nearly all the time was waiting on Pulumi, which ruled out a whole class of experiments.

*Keep the constraint in the prompt.* "Don't change the stacks, the resources or the state" did a lot of work. Several experiments were faster and wrong. Turning off server-side apply broke previews. Trusting provided secrets over live ones sped things up but proposed replacing databases. Because of the constraint, the loop checked for changed diffs and threw those out.

*It'll touch more than the code.* Mine changed my DNS settings, with my permission. Decide up front what it's allowed to touch outside the repo.

Four days and 181 experiments took a preview that wouldn't finish, then took five and a half minutes, to one that runs in about half a minute. Most of the work was done by a loop that didn't get bored.
