AI didn't make code review unnecessary. It made code review the bottleneck.

Once everyone on the team had agents writing code, the number of pull requests went up and their size went up with it. [NUMBER: PRs a week, or lines per PR, before and after agents] The number of people who can review them didn't change. Reviews got slower, then shallower. A reviewer with ten open PRs skims.

We're not unusual. In May, Meta published [[https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30208][a paper on RADAR]], the system they built to auto-review low-risk changes. Their numbers: lines of code per human-landed diff up 106% year over year, diffs per developer up 51%, and agentic AI behind over 80% of that growth. Same problem, at a much bigger scale.

So I built Radar. It started as a Go implementation of that paper. It's grown into the AI reviewer on every pull request at work, and it's on GitHub at [[https://github.com/travisjeffery/radar][travisjeffery/radar]].

This is the first post in a series. This one covers why, how it works, and what it does. The next one covers how we cut what it costs to run.

** What the paper does

RADAR stands for Risk Aware Diff Auto Review. The idea is a funnel. Every change goes through layered gates, and only one that passes all of them skips human review. Anything that fails a gate goes to a person.

The gates, roughly:

- *Static checks.* Is the change in scope? Do CI and its state pass? Does it touch a blocklisted path or contain blocklisted content?
- *A risk score.* Meta has a model that scores each diff's risk, the Diff Risk Score. Scores are calibrated to percentiles, so a threshold like P20 means "only the safest 20% qualify."
- *An AI reviewer.* An LLM reviews the change and must be confident with zero risk signals.

Meta reports that RADAR reviewed over 535,000 diffs and landed over 331,000. Diffs it reviewed were reverted a third as often as others, and caused a fiftieth as many production incidents. Those numbers come from their scale and their risk model. I can't reproduce them and don't claim to.

My first version was that funnel: the gates, the decisions and the metrics, with the two proprietary pieces (the risk model and the AI reviewer) as interfaces. That's still in the repo as ~radar classify~ and ~radar replay~.

** What changed when it met real PRs

Pointed at our pull requests, a few things became clear.

The risk score didn't hold up. Without Meta's model, my stand-in score is mostly size, and gating on size just means big changes always go to a person, whatever they contain. Now approval rests on what the reviewers find, not on how big the change is.

The review mattered more than the verdict. People read the review. If it was going to be on every PR, it had to be a good review in its own right, not a yes or no. So Radar became the AI review first, and an approver second.

And approval had to earn its way in. Radar ran in comment-only mode for about a week, with its approvals in shadow, before anyone considered letting it approve. Shadow is still the default. Radar never merges a pull request and never requests changes.

** How it works

Radar runs in GitHub Actions as two stages on each pull request head.

*Findings.* As soon as a PR opens or changes, Radar reviews it, without waiting for checks. It posts one comment with the findings grouped by severity, and the P0s and P1s inline.

*Verdict.* Once checks finish, Radar decides. It reuses the findings stage's reviews when the request is byte-identical, so the comment people read and the decision rest on the same reviews, and the verdict normally needs no new model call.

Each review goes to more than one model. At work that's Claude on Bedrock and Codex, run concurrently from the same snapshot. Both can read the repository, not just the patch: Codex runs read-only over a checkout of the head, and Claude gets read-only tools (~read_file~, ~grep~, ~list_dir~, ~git_log~) confined to the checkout. Their findings are merged. Two findings in the same file, within three lines, citing the same rule are one issue, and the comment shows who agreed.

The part I'd keep if I threw everything else away: *the model is never asked to approve.* Every reviewer gets the same prompt and returns a structured review: a summary, findings with severity, file, lines, impact and a fix, questions, assumptions, suggested tests, and what it covered. Radar computes the verdict from those fields. P0s and P1s block. So do defect signals, a structural change, a reviewer that didn't cover every file, and paths or phrases the policy denies. The comment is rendered from the same data, so the verdict can't disagree with what the review says.

Everything fails closed. A provider error, a timeout, a response cut off, checks that change between two reads, a policy that doesn't load: any of these routes to a person.

** The features

Most of these exist because something went wrong without them.

- *Severity measures what the change does.* Early on, the findings people thumbs-downed were all P0s and P1s, and few were plain false positives. Severity was wrong, not the finding. Now a P0 or P1 needs a line this PR changes to create or newly expose the risk. A gap the change only passes near is pre-existing: a P2 and a question for the author.
- *Review rounds converge.* Every push gets a full review, and at first any new P1 blocked. PRs never finished: each round found something new, usually on lines the author hadn't touched. From round two, a P1 blocks only if it's on or near lines changed since the last review. Anything raised late on unchanged lines is shown apart, collapsed, and doesn't block.
- *You can answer it.* Reply to a finding with ~/radar~ and what it missed. Radar re-checks just that finding. It withdraws or downgrades it only if the re-check cites lines that exist at the head. If the only evidence is your word, the finding stops blocking but the PR goes to a person. Authors can't talk Radar into approving their own change.
- *PR text is data, not instructions.* The title, description, commits, linked issue, open threads and check states all go to the reviewers, inside one delimited, length-capped block. The prompt says it's untrusted, and to report anything that tries to steer the review. The verdict never reads it, so injected text can't approve anything.
- *Per-area criteria.* A rules file maps paths to review packs (rules with IDs the findings cite) and to policy, like "IAM changes always need a person." Area policy can only tighten. The rules, packs and guidance come from the default branch, never the PR, so a change can't rewrite what it's reviewed against.
- *Generated files and lockfiles.* Generated files are withheld from the reviewers, but only ones that already existed at a generated path, so naming a new file like generated code doesn't hide it. Lockfiles are summarized per package, and supply-chain signals (a new source, a downgrade, a hash-only change, a lockfile change with no manifest change) are P1s or go straight to a person.
- *A person can always insist.* The ~radar:human-review~ label routes a PR to a person. Only people with write or triage access can set labels, so unlike text in the description, it's trusted.
- *Approval stays current.* In approve mode, a Radar approval must not carry over to code pushed after it. Dismissing approvals on push would dismiss human approvals too, so Radar keeps a commit status current on each head, and the branch rules require it.
- *Review before the PR.* ~radar-gh review-commit~ reviews a pushed commit exactly as CI would review a PR from it. A hosted MCP server runs the same review from Claude Code or claude.ai, so you can ask for Radar's review before you open the PR.

** The sharp edges

- *AI review isn't human review.* Radar finds a lot. It doesn't know why the change exists or whether it's the right change. That's still a person's job, and the review is written to tell that person where to look.
- *Models disagree with themselves.* The same PR reviewed twice can get different findings. Merging several reviewers and computing the verdict from structured fields makes that tolerable, not gone.
- *Calibration is ongoing.* Most of the rules above came from reading findings people disagreed with. Severity and convergence fixes have fixtures from real, anonymised rounds so they don't regress.
- *Approval is a separate decision.* Comment-only is useful on its own and low-risk to turn on. Letting a bot approve needs branch rules you trust, a shadow period, and someone who owns the outcome.

** Why build it

What I needed was a reviewer that keeps up with how fast we write code now, that people actually read, and that never approves something because a model said "looks good."

AI made each of us faster at writing code. Reviewing it has to get faster too, or the speed just piles up in the review queue. Radar takes the first pass on every change so people spend their review time where it matters.
