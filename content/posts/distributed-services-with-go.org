I wrote a book, [[https://pragprog.com/titles/tjgo/distributed-services-with-go/][Distributed Services with Go]], published by the Pragmatic Bookshelf. It's a practical book on distributed systems. It walks you through building a distributed event-stream service from scratch through deployment, and along the way it explains the concepts underlying the distributed systems you use, work on, or plan to build. The code is at [[https://github.com/travisjeffery/proglog][travisjeffery/proglog]].

I spent about two and a half years writing it. In this post I'll share what that was like, and whether it was worth it.

I've been a bookworm as long as I can remember. I have over 1,200 books in my personal library. Writing one was a lifelong goal, and I wanted to test myself and see if I could do it.

I also wanted to write the book I'd wanted to read: a practical book on distributed systems that you could read and come out the other side having built a distributed service. A lot of technical books today are basically glorified man pages. I wanted to write one with content that didn't exist yet, and a really good one at that.

** Publisher and Plan

In April 2018, Tyler Treat introduced me to Brian MacDonald, an editor at the Pragmatic Bookshelf. Pragmatic was looking for authors to write a Go book. I told them I didn't want to write a book on learning Go. There are already a lot of those, and The Go Programming Language will always be the standard. Brian agreed. He thought all the learning Go books we need had been written, and he was looking for books on how to solve real problems with Go. That's the kind of writing I love reading and writing most.

Pragmatic accepted my proposal in June 2018, and I signed the contract in July. The working title was /Real-World Go/. In August my introduction still had a heading that read "Why Real-World Go? (TK: title TBD)". By late September Pragmatic was calling it /Distributed Services with Go/, and in October I put that title in the book's info file, though the managing editor's review in January 2019 still called it /Real-World Go/. That month I told my editor I thought /Distributed Services with Go/ made more sense. Part of our trouble was mixed expectations: people expected a book about Go the language, and I'd planned with Brian to write a book about building something with Go. The book's own build files said /Real-World Go/ until the first beta in March 2020, which also added the subtitle, /Your Guide to Reliable, Scalable, and Maintainable Systems/.

My first development editor was Jackie Carter. In September 2018, Susan Conant, the managing editor, moved me to Dawn Schanafelt, and the plan had the full draft done in early 2019.

PragProg is one of the publishers whose books I like most. Their style is relaxed and practical. I hadn't worked with a publisher or professional editors before, and I was pleasantly surprised. They have an internal wiki full of useful info and an author guide that's 250 pages long, like a style guide. Half of it is on how to structure and prepare to write your book. The other half is on the syntax to format it. I liked the structure it gave me.

You can write in either Markdown or Pragmatic's own HTML-like markup, PML. I wrote in Markdown, in Emacs, with =pragmatic-mode=, a mode I wrote for myself to help with editing.

** Tough Beginning

The book was slow going for the first six months.

I was already working full-time at Confluent, building Confluent Cloud from zero to one, and it was only about a year and a half old. Adding another whole job of writing a book was a bit insane. I'd also gone from Brian to Jackie to Dawn in my first three months, and each of them gave me different advice.

I went into it with the idea, which I think came from 37signals, that writing a book is like writing 10-15 blog posts, one for each chapter. That's somewhat true for 37signals because their chapters are generally 1-3 pages long and don't build on each other. It isn't true for most books, and definitely not mine. My book has the reader begin with nothing and build a distributed service from scratch, so each chapter leads directly to the next. If I made a change to chapter 10, I had to back port it to the related content in chapters 1-9. And each chapter is 10-15 pages long. I never write a blog post that long.

** Turning Point

The big turning point was about motivation. My editors were helpful with editing, of course, but probably their biggest help was motivation. In October 2018, my editor sent me an email that was essentially asking, "are you going to get it going or what?" That email was the turning point. Two weeks later I had a call with Susan, and I committed to sending Dawn a chapter a week. I had about 5,300 words in August 2018 and about 19,500 by the end of the year.

Getting going didn't mean the writing was good yet. In November 2018, Dawn said the book read more like a lecture than a conversation, and that I should make the reader the star of the book and take them on a hero's journey. Brian had sent me /Programming Elm/ as an example of a reader-centric book, one where the author introduces every bit of code with phrases like "add this code."

Pragmatic does a three-chapter check early on, where the managing editor reads your first chapters. Mine started on January 16, 2019, and the feedback came back on January 21. Susan said my writing felt forced, the topics had bare-minimum coverage, and there was little sense of my voice. It read more like a handout from a Go conference tutorial. The fix meant turning one chapter into an appendix and writing a new chapter to replace it. You can see it in the word count: about 19,500 words at the end of 2018 and about 19,700 two months later.

I sent Dawn the new chapter 1 on February 7, and the tone changed completely. On February 21, Susan and Brian agreed it was much improved. Susan's note on it was: "This feels like Travis. I have a sense of him now and readers will too." I started writing new chapters in that more conversational style. My first chapters needed the same overhaul. On May 1, Dawn reset the count of edited chapters from 6 of 13 back to 2.

** Flow state

I really picked up the pace after that, and my pace kept improving each year until the end. Here's the book's approximate word count over time, from the repository's history:

Aug 2018     5,300

#+end_src

The flat spot in early 2019 is the three-chapter check. The dip in fall 2019 is cuts, while the first half was in technical review.

I also wrote about twice what was printed. Across all my edits I added roughly 106,000 words and deleted roughly 57,000, for a book of about 55,000 words.

Writing a book is tough. Really, really tough. People say writing a book is like running a marathon. It isn't. It's running one marathon after another, one for each chapter in your book, plus another marathon for all the other stuff. You go through the grueling process of writing a chapter, send it off to your editor, take a brief moment to celebrate finishing another chapter, and then realize you have many more to go and you'd better begin on the next. The whole time, you have only yourself, with some help from your editor, to provide the motivation and discipline to keep going.

There are pretty much no places I compromised. I could tinker on minor things forever, arguing with myself over the upsides and downsides of each possible change. But after two years, if I wasn't happy with something in the book, I changed it until I was happy or threw it in the trash.

A lot went in the trash. I cut a whole chapter on writing a protobuf plugin. The testing chapters became testing sections in the other chapters. Chapters on Kubernetes operators, performance, and the CLI never got past an outline. The plan went from 8 chapters, up to 15, and down to the 11 the book shipped with.

My writing days were mostly writing nights. I had a full-time job, so the book got the hours around it. About two-thirds of my commits were between 10pm and 6am, and my busiest hour was 5am. Monday was my busiest day and Saturday my quietest. About a fifth of my commits landed on weekends. When I went quiet, Dawn checked in, like on May 29 and July 24, 2019.

Halfway through, I changed editors. In July 2019 Dawn told me she was pregnant, and in September she said she'd hand the book to another editor once we hit the halfway mark. In November 2019, Katie Dvorak took over for the rest of the book. Dawn got me through the rewrite and the first half. Katie got me through the second half, the betas, and production.

Clinton Begin reviewed the preface. He caught that I edged on insulting the authors of many open source frameworks and tools, and at one point referred to their code as spaghetti code. He suggested I make the same point by saying their codebases are very large and not focused on learning. I didn't take that one. "Spaghetti code" is in the printed book.

** Release

At Pragmatic, a book goes into beta at about the three-quarters mark and begins selling on their website. That's when you begin making money. My book went into beta on March 25, 2020. Each beta added chapters:

#+begin_src
Beta 1   March 25, 2020       178 pages
Beta 2   June 16, 2020        207 pages
Beta 3   August 12, 2020      213 pages
Beta 4   September 23, 2020   224 pages
Print    March 2021           245 pages
#+end_src

The final production work ran into 2021: indexing in January, then layout review in March. The book went to the printer in March 2021 and was published at the end of the month.

The relationship with a publisher can be frustrating. You put everything into your book, so when they let you down on something small, like updating a marketing page, it's quite frustrating.

Release day wasn't the big day for me. The big day was when I held a copy in my hands. My copies showed up on July 5, 2021, months after the book came out, because of a mix-up with my shipping address. After all that writing, it was finally a real book.

Writing a book is probably the least efficient way to make money. Most of this timeline is from before AI took off, and from what I understand AI has crashed technical book sales. If you were just in it for the money, you'd never finish, so you're setting yourself up for failure if you write a book for the money.

I wrote the book because I wanted to write it, and before that I'd wanted to read it. It was as good a way as any to accomplish my lifelong goal of writing a book. By the criteria of my own fulfillment, and all the messages I've received from people thanking me for writing it, writing the book was worth it.

Was the publisher worth it? Self-publishing is a viable option today. You could put your book on Leanpub, hire your own editor, hire an artist for the cover or make your own, get it printed, and get it into stores. Partly I went with a publisher because that's how it naturally began and the momentum kept it going. But I think if you don't care about the money, you should use a publisher:

- Structure and resources: editors, author guides, conventions.
- Marketing and an existing audience.
- Convenience. I wrote this book as a side gig to a full-time job, and by the end of just writing it I'd had enough. If I'd had to do all that other stuff too, it would have sucked.

I have a decent following, so I think my sales would have been similar if I'd self-published. But my book is better for having worked with a publisher and editors. There's a good chance I would've never finished it at all.

Would I write another book? I'd write another edition of this one when it made sense. I haven't started one. I won't write a whole new book for a while, but I think I will eventually. I think I'll write a fiction book some day as well.
